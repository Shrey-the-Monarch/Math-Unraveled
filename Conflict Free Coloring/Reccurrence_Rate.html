The Exact Size of the Conflict-Free Gadget Hierarchy

A closed form and matching growth rate for \(|G_k|\)

Author

Shrey Shah

Published

September 5, 2026

Abstract

Abel et al. (2018) construct a family of graphs \(G_k\) with \(\chi_{CF}(G_k) = k\), used as the forcing gadget in their proof that conflict-free coloring is NP-complete for every fixed \(k \ge 3\). They bound the size of this family between \(\Omega(2^k)\) and \(\mathcal{O}(2^{k \log k})\). We give a closed form for \(|G_k|\) by reducing the size recurrence to a Pell recurrence, and show \(|G_k| = \Theta\!\left(k!\,(1+\sqrt{2})^{k}\right)\), with the normalized ratio \(|G_k| / (k!\,\alpha^k)\) converging to an explicit constant. The rate is consistent with both published bounds and strictly sharper than either.

1 Setup

Sections Section 2 through Section 8 are reproduced verbatim from the longer walkthrough. This section is the only new material, and exists to make the note self-contained.

We recall the two definitions the result depends on, and nothing else.

Definition 1 (Conflict-free coloring) A partial \(k\)-coloring of \(G\) is an assignment \(\chi : V' \to \{1, \dots, k\}\) for some subset \(V' \subseteq V(G)\); vertices outside \(V'\) are uncolored. Such a \(\chi\) is conflict-free with respect to closed neighborhoods if for every \(v \in V(G)\) there is a vertex \(w \in N[v] \cap V'\) whose color is unique in \(N[v]\) — that is, \(\chi(w') \neq \chi(w)\) for every other \(w' \in N[v] \cap V'\). We call \(w\) the conflict-free neighbor of \(v\). The conflict-free chromatic number \(\chi_{CF}(G)\) is the smallest \(k\) for which such a coloring exists.

The family \(G_k\) is built recursively, with \(G_1\) a single vertex and \(G_2\) the star \(K_{1,3}\) with one edge subdivided. Given \(G_k\) and \(G_{k-1}\):

  • Take a complete graph \(G = K_{k+1}\) on \(k+1\) vertices.
  • To each vertex \(v \in V(K_{k+1})\) attach two disjoint and independent copies of \(G_k\), adding an edge from every \(v\) to every vertex of both copies of \(G_k\).
  • For each edge \(e = vw \in E(K_{k+1})\), add two disjoint and independent copies of \(G_{k-1}\), where there is an edge from every \(v\) and \(w\) to every vertex on both copies of \(G_{k-1}\).

This gives \(\chi_{CF}(G_k) = k\) for all \(k \ge 1\). Only the vertex counts matter below.

2 Counting the vertices

\[|G_1| = 1, \qquad |G_2| = 5, \qquad |G_3| = 3 + 3\big(2|G_2|\big) + 3\big(2|G_1|\big) = 3 + 30 + 6 = 39 .\]

In general, the core contributes \(k+1\) vertices, each of the \(k+1\) core vertices carries \(2|G_k|\), and each of the \(\binom{k+1}{2}\) core edges carries \(2|G_{k-1}|\). Since

\[\binom{k+1}{2} = \frac{(k+1)!}{2!\,(k-1)!} = \frac{k(k+1)}{2},\]

we get

\[|G_{k+1}| \;=\; \underbrace{(k+1)}_{v \in K_{k+1}} \;+\; \underbrace{2(k+1)\,|G_k|}_{H \text{ graphs}} \;+\; \underbrace{k(k+1)\,|G_{k-1}|}_{J \text{ graphs}}\]

and factoring out \((k+1)\),

\[\boxed{\;|G_{k+1}| = (k+1)\Big(2|G_k| + k\,|G_{k-1}| + 1\Big).\;}\]

Check: \(|G_3| = 3(2\cdot 5 + 2\cdot 1 + 1) = 3 \cdot 13 = 39\) and \(|G_4| = 4(2 \cdot 39 + 3 \cdot 5 + 1) = 4 \cdot 94 = 376\).

3 Algorithmic bounds

Every term of the bracket is positive, so \(a_{k+1} \ge 2a_k\), and therefore

\[a_k = \Omega\!\left(2^k\right).\]

This is the bound stated in the paper, and it is all that is needed there. We can do better by keeping the factor in front: since \(a_{k+1} \ge 2(k+1)a_k\), unrolling from \(a_2 = 5\) gives

\[a_k \;\ge\; 5 \prod_{j=3}^{k} 2j \;=\; 5 \cdot 2^{k-2} \cdot \frac{k!}{2},\]

so \(\log_2 a_k = \Omega(k \log k)\).

The sequence is increasing, so \(a_{k-1} \le a_k\), and also \(1 \le a_k\). Hence

\[a_{k+1} \;\le\; (k+1)\big(2a_k + k\,a_k + a_k\big) \;=\; (k+1)(k+3)\,a_k \;\le\; (k+3)^2 a_k .\]

Unrolling from \(a_1 = 1\),

\[a_{k+1} \;\le\; \prod_{j=1}^{k} (j+3)^2 \;\le\; (k+3)^{2k},\]

and taking logarithms,

\[\log_2 a_{k+1} \;\le\; 2k \log_2 (k+3) \;=\; O(k \log k),\]

so \(a_k = \mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{k \log k}\right)\).

3.1 Solving the recurrence

First we rewrite our recurrence as a sequence:

\[a_1 = 1, \qquad a_2 = 5, \qquad a_{k+1} = (k+1)\big(2a_k + k\,a_{k-1} + 1\big), \qquad k, a \in \mathbb{Z}^+ .\]

The \((k+1)\) out front and the \(k\) on the second term hide a Pell recurrence. We divide it out.

4 Normalizing away the factorials

Divide both sides by \((k+1)!\):

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{(k+1)!} \;=\; \frac{2a_k + k\,a_{k-1} + 1}{k!} .\]

Let \(b_k = \dfrac{a_k}{k!}\). The middle term simplifies because \(\dfrac{k\,a_{k-1}}{k!} = \dfrac{a_{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = b_{k-1}\), so

\[b_{k+1} \;=\; 2b_k + b_{k-1} + \frac{1}{k!} .\]

Isolating the \(1/k!\), what is left is exactly the Pell recurrence

\[b_{k+1} = 2b_k + b_{k-1} .\]

The seeds come from the graph: \(b_1 = \dfrac{a_1}{1!} = 1\), where \(a_1\) is the number of vertices in \(G_1\), and \(b_2 = \dfrac{a_2}{2!} = \dfrac{5}{2}\), referencing \(K_{1,3}\) for \(a_2\).

5 The Pell recurrence

\[P_n = 2P_{n-1} + P_{n-2}, \qquad P_0 = 0,\; P_1 = 1, \qquad \langle 0,1,2,5,12,29,70,\dots\rangle\]

Set \(P_n = r^n\), so \(r^n = 2r^{n-1} + r^{n-2}\), giving \(r^2 = 2r + 1\) and

\[r^2 - 2r - 1 = 0, \qquad r = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4+4}}{2} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{8}}{2}, \qquad r = 1 + \sqrt{2},\; r = 1 - \sqrt{2}.\]

The recurrence is linear, so

\[P_n = A\big(1+\sqrt{2}\big)^n + B\big(1-\sqrt{2}\big)^n\]

where \(A\) and \(B\) satisfy all values of the recurrence. From \(P_0 = 0\) we get \(A = -B\), and from \(P_1 = 1\),

\[1 = A\big(1+\sqrt2\big) - A\big(1-\sqrt2\big) = A\big(1 + \sqrt2 + \sqrt2 - 1\big) = A \cdot 2\sqrt2 ,\]

so \(A = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}\) and \(B = \dfrac{-1}{2\sqrt2}\), giving

\[P_n = \frac{\big(1+\sqrt{2}\big)^n - \big(1-\sqrt{2}\big)^n}{2\sqrt{2}} .\]

5.1 The homogeneous part

Running the Pell recurrence forward from \(b_1, b_2\):

\[b_3 = 2b_2 + b_1, \qquad b_4 = 2b_3 + b_2 = 5b_2 + 2b_1, \qquad b_5 = 2b_4 + b_3 = 12b_2 + 5b_1 .\]

The coefficients are Pell numbers, so we generalize:

\[b_k \;=\; P_{k-1}\,b_2 + P_{k-2}\,b_1 \;=\; \frac{5}{2}P_{k-1} + P_{k-2} .\]

5.2 The inhomogeneous part

Our actual recurrence has \(1/k!\) added, so

\[b_3 = 6 + \frac{1}{2!} = 6.5, \qquad b_4 = 2b_3 + b_2 + \frac{1}{3!} = 5b_2 + 2b_1 + \frac{1}{3!} + 2\left(\frac{1}{2!}\right),\]

\[b_5 = 2b_4 + b_3 + \frac{1}{4!} = 12b_2 + 5b_1 + 2\left(\frac{1}{3!}\right) + 5\left(\frac{1}{2!}\right) + \frac{1}{4!} .\]

Each factorial creates its own Pell: a term \(\dfrac{1}{j!}\) injected at step \(j\) propagates forward with Pell coefficients, contributing \(\dfrac{P_{k-j}}{j!}\) to \(b_k\).

Verification. Let \(k = 5\), \(j = 2\). We have \(\dfrac{P_3}{2!}\), where \(P_3 = 5\), so \(\dfrac{5}{2!}\) — which is the coefficient appearing on \(\dfrac{1}{2!}\) in \(b_5\) above.

Accounting for every \(j\), for some \(k\):

\[\sum_{j=2}^{k-1} \frac{P_{k-j}}{j!} .\]

6 The closed form

Solving for our altered Pell recurrence:

\[b_k \;=\; \frac{5P_{k-1}}{2} + P_{k-2} + \sum_{j=2}^{k-1} \frac{P_{k-j}}{j!} .\]

Remember that \(b_k = \dfrac{a_k}{k!}\), and that \(a_k\) is just our actual recurrence sequentialized, so

\[\boxed{\;|G_k| \;=\; k!\left[\,P_{k-2} + \frac{5P_{k-1}}{2} + \sum_{j=2}^{k-1} \frac{P_{k-j}}{j!}\,\right]\;}\]

with \(P_n\) as above. Using the standard backward extension \(P_{-1} = 1\), this is valid for all \(k \ge 1\).

Check. \(k = 3\) gives \(6\left[2 + \tfrac52 + \tfrac{P_1}{2!}\right] = 6(6.5) = 39\), and \(k = 4\) gives \(24\left[5 + \tfrac{25}{2} + \tfrac{P_2}{2!} + \tfrac{P_1}{3!}\right] = 24\left(\tfrac{47}{3}\right) = 376\), matching the direct counts. The sequence continues \(1,\,5,\,39,\,376,\,4545,\,65826,\,1112461,\dots\)

7 Growth rate

Write \(\alpha = 1+\sqrt2\) and \(\beta = 1-\sqrt2\), so

\[P_n = \frac{\alpha^n - \beta^n}{2\sqrt2} .\]

Since \(|\beta| = \sqrt2 - 1 < 1\), the \(\beta^n\) term vanishes and

\[\lim_{n \to \infty} P_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\alpha^n}{2\sqrt2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad P_n = \Theta\!\left(\alpha^n\right) = \Theta\!\left(\big(1+\sqrt2\big)^n\right).\]

For the sum, \(P_{k-j} = \Theta\!\left(\alpha^{k-j}\right)\), so

\[\sum_{j=2}^{k-1} \frac{P_{k-j}}{j!} \;=\; \Theta\!\left(\sum_{j=2}^{k-1} \frac{\alpha^{k}}{j!\,\alpha^{j}}\right) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(\alpha^{k} \sum_{j=2}^{k-1} \frac{1}{j!\,\alpha^{j}}\right),\]

where \(\sum_{j\ge2} \dfrac{1}{j!\,\alpha^{j}}\) converges — to \(e^{1/\alpha} - 1 - \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\) — so the whole sum is \(\Theta\!\left(\alpha^k\right)\). (The \(j=2\) term alone is already \(\Theta(\alpha^k)\), which gives the matching lower bound.)

The remaining pieces \(\dfrac{5P_{k-1}}{2}\) and \(P_{k-2}\) are Pell recurrences multiplied by the constants \(\dfrac{5}{2\alpha}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{\alpha^2}\) respectively, so they are both \(\Theta\!\left(\alpha^k\right)\) as well.

So \(\dfrac{|G_k|}{k!} = \Theta\!\left(\big(1+\sqrt2\big)^k\right)\), and thus

\[\boxed{\;|G_k| \;=\; \Theta\!\left(k!\,\big(1+\sqrt{2}\big)^{k}\right).\;}\]

Because every piece has the same growth constant, the ratio actually converges rather than merely being bounded:

\[\lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{|G_k|}{k!\,\alpha^k} \;=\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\left(e^{\sqrt2 - 1} + \frac{1-\sqrt2}{2}\right) \;\approx\; 0.4617667 ,\]

using \(1/\alpha = \sqrt2 - 1\).

8 Comparison with the paper

The paper states only that \(|G_k|\) is in \(\Omega\!\left(2^k\right)\) and \(\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{k \log k}\right)\), for fixed \(k\) the gadget has constant size, which is what makes the reduction in Lemma 3.4 run in polynomial time. The exact rate is consistent with both bounds, since \(\log_2\!\left(k!\,\alpha^k\right) = \Theta(k \log k)\), but the closed form derived in this document along with it’s growth raate are strictly sharper because we pin the base at \(1 + \sqrt2 \approx 2.414\)

What the sharper rate buys. The constant hidden in the reduction of (Abel et al. 2018, Lemma 3.4) is \(C_1 = 2|G_k|\). Under the closed form this is \(\Theta\!\left(k!\,(1+\sqrt2)^k\right)\), already \(752\) at \(k = 4\). The reduction is therefore linear for each fixed \(k\) and catastrophic in \(k\), which is why the result is a statement about CF \(k\)-Coloring for each constant \(k\) rather than about the problem with \(k\) given as part of the input.
